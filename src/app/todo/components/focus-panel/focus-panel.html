Importance outranks urgency. A must-have stays above a could-have no matter how soon the could-have is due. People reliably pick the urgent task over the more valuable one, even when both fit the same slot — Zhu, Yang & Hsee, The Mere Urgency Effect (Journal of Consumer Research, 2018). Urgency only orders todos of equal importance.

A day matters more than a decision. Naming when something happens raises follow-through far more than resolving to do it — Gollwitzer & Sheeran's meta-analysis of 94 studies on implementation intentions (2006). That is why must-haves without a day get their own line.

Your own past beats your estimate. We underestimate what a day holds; the working remedy is the outside view — Buehler, Griffin & Ross on the planning fallacy (1994). The count above compares today's plan with the median you actually finished on earlier planned days, and stays quiet until there are enough of them to say anything.

Marking importance: start a todo's title with /must or /could. Anything else counts as normal.

Not used: the Eisenhower matrix. It is management folklore without primary research — its useful half is the first point above.
