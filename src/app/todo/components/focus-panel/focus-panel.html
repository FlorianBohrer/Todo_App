Importance outranks urgency. A must-have stays above a could-have no matter how soon the could-have is due. People reliably pick the urgent task over the more valuable one, even when both fit the same slot. See Zhu, Yang & Hsee, The Mere Urgency Effect (Journal of Consumer Research, 2018). Urgency only orders todos of equal importance.

A day matters more than a decision. Naming when something happens raises follow-through far more than resolving to do it. See Gollwitzer & Sheeran's meta-analysis of 94 studies on implementation intentions (2006). That is why must-haves without a day get their own line.

Your own past beats your estimate. We underestimate what a day holds; the working remedy is the outside view. See Buehler, Griffin & Ross on the planning fallacy (1994). The count above compares today's plan with the median you actually finished on earlier planned days, and stays quiet until there are enough of them to say anything.

MoSCoW. Start a todo's title with /must, /should, /could or /won't. The names carry definitions, not just a ranking: a must-have makes the delivery worthless if it is missing; a should-have hurts to leave out but has a workaround; a could-have is the contingency you drop when time runs short; a won't-have is deliberately out of scope for now, recorded as a decision rather than forgotten. Won't-haves are left out of the ranking above for exactly that reason.

Not used: the Eisenhower matrix. It is management folklore without primary research. Its useful half is the first point above.
